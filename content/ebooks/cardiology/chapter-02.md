# Chapter 2: Heart Failure Fundamentals

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome, not a single disease — it's the
final common pathway for many cardiac insults. Understanding it starts with
classifying by ejection fraction, because that classification drives
therapy.

## Classification

- **HFrEF** (reduced ejection fraction, EF ≤ 40%): a problem of contractility.
- **HFmrEF** (mildly reduced, EF 41–49%): an intermediate group, increasingly
  treated similarly to HFrEF.
- **HFpEF** (preserved ejection fraction, EF ≥ 50%): a problem of relaxation
  and filling (diastolic dysfunction), often driven by hypertension,
  obesity, and age.

## Recognizing decompensation

Look for the congestive picture:

- Dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
- Elevated JVP, hepatojugular reflux
- Bibasilar crackles, S3 gallop
- Peripheral edema
- Weight gain over days (fluid retention, not fat)

## Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HFrEF

Four pillars, all shown to reduce mortality, ideally started together and
up-titrated as tolerated rather than sequentially:

1. **ARNI** (sacubitril-valsartan) — or an ACE inhibitor/ARB if ARNI isn't
   feasible
2. **Beta-blocker** — carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or bisoprolol only
   (not all beta-blockers have mortality data in HF)
3. **Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist** — spironolactone or eplerenone
4. **SGLT2 inhibitor** — dapagliflozin or empagliflozin, benefit
   independent of diabetes status

Loop diuretics (furosemide, torsemide) manage congestion and symptoms but do
not reduce mortality — they're symptomatic therapy layered on top of GDMT.

## HFpEF management

Fewer proven mortality-reducing therapies. Focus on:

- Aggressive blood pressure control
- SGLT2 inhibitors (shown to reduce HF hospitalization)
- Diuretics for volume management
- Treating underlying causes (AFib, obesity, sleep apnea)

## Key teaching point

An S3 gallop in an older adult usually signals volume overload / systolic
dysfunction; an S4 usually signals a stiff, hypertrophied ventricle (e.g.,
long-standing hypertension) rather than acute decompensation.
